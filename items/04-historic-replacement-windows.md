# Case 4 - Historically Accurate Replacement Windows - Looks ineligible, but is

## Scenario

Raspberry Park is a fictional New Jersey shore town of about 6,000 year-round residents. The borough owns The Rabbits Center, an Art Deco style, 4-story brick-and-terra-cotta convention hall built in 1929 and listed on the National Register of Historic Places. The Center stands directly over the boardwalk, and is less than 50 feet from the ocean.

The Center is in a VE zone on the current Flood Insurance Rate Map, with a Base Flood Elevation of 13 feet (NAVD88). Its first floor is at 10 feet, and its basement holds the electrical switchgear, boilers, and the emergency generator. Seawater flooded the basement in a 2012 hurricane and again in a 2022 nor’easter. The 2022 event knocked out power to the building for nine days.

The borough has designated the Center as its post-storm supply distribution and recovery center. It’s in good standing with the NFIP and holds flood insurance on the Center. The site isn’t within a Coastal Barrier Resources System unit or an Otherwise Protected Area.

The borough administrator wants to “restore and harden The Rabbits Center for the next century” and “make the boardwalk worthy of it.” The borough plans to apply under the FY2024 & 2025 BRIC NOFO, with applications open March 25 – July 23, 2026. No construction has started on any project below.

### Facts

The Rabbits Center, built in 1929, features Art Deco style windows. The Rabbits Center is one of the most well-known Art Deco style buildings in America, alongside The Chrysler Building and the Empire State Building in New York City. The Rabbits Center windows are iconic for their colored stained glass depicting important events in early 20th Century New Jersey history, and feature large plate glass, slim metal frames, vertically recessed groupings, and intricate geometric and stained glass details. A Raspberry Park engineer who is passionate about historic preservation insists that it should be possible to request BRIC funding to restore and replace these older, fragile windows with impact-rated units made to match the 1929 profiles and designs. Other engineers have determined this looks more like a historic restoration project and would not be funded by the BRIC program

## Governing text

Department of Homeland Security (DHS) Notice of Funding Opportunity (NOFO), for FY24 and FY25 “Building Resilient Infrastructure and Communities”

Federal Emergency Management Agency (FEMA)’s Hazard Mitigation Assistance (HMA) Program and Policy Guide, effective January 20, 2025

## Position

Another engineer on the team against the window restoration argues,  ““The window project isn’t fundable under BRIC, because custom replicas of the 1929 Art Deco windows and their stained glass are historic restoration, and FEMA doesn’t pay for restoring or beautifying old buildings.”

Goal: Determine which costs of the window project BRIC can fund.

## Arguments

### Argument 1

FEMA’s ban on restoration only applies to work done without an increase in the level of protection. Replacing the 1929 glass for impact-rated units raises protection against windborne debris, so the restoration exclusion doesn’t reach this project. This would constitute a Wind Retrofit project that protects openings to resist high winds, windborne debris, and wind-driven rain. Excerpt from FEMA HMA Program and Policy Guide, pg. 50: “Projects that, without an increase in the level of protection, address the operation, deferred or future maintenance, rehabilitation, restoration or replacement of existing structures, facilities or infrastructure (e.g., … maintenance/rehabilitation of facilities, including dams and other flood risk reduction structures)” are deemed ineligible for BRIC funding.

### Argument 2

The windows probably can’t use FEMA’s pre-calculated benefits, which means the town will likely need a full benefit-cost analysis, and the extra cost of the historic replicas will count against it. For non-residential buildings, the pre-calculated benefit only covers opening protection when it’s paired with a roof retrofit, and only for buildings framed in steel, concrete, or reinforced masonry. That limits how the town can prove the project is cost-effective, but it doesn’t make the windows ineligible. Pairing the windows with the roof work already listed in the hazard mitigation plan would help. Excerpt from FEMA HMA Program and Policy Guide, pg. 463: “Option 1: Opening protection for doors, windows and louvers against wind-borne debris, wind-driven rain and high wind pressure; and roof retrofit, which consists of securing rooftop equipment, replacing roof decking and covering … Eligible structures must have primary structural framing that consists of steel, concrete or reinforced masonry. Structures composed of other building materials, such as wood, steel stud and unreinforced masonry, are not eligible for this pre-calculated benefit.”

### Argument 3

Only a few studios in the country can still fabricate period-accurate Art Deco stained glass, so the replicas will have long lead times and high bids. BRIC eligibility depends on whether a project reduces damage from natural hazards and is cost-effective, and the cost of these windows will likely deem this component of the project unallowable under the program.
