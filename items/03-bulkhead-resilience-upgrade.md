# Case 3 - Bulkhead Resilience Upgrade - Looks eligible but isn’t

## Scenario

Raspberry Park is a fictional New Jersey shore town of about 6,000 year-round residents. The borough owns The Rabbits Center, an Art Deco style, 4-story brick-and-terra-cotta convention hall built in 1929 and listed on the National Register of Historic Places. The Center stands directly over the boardwalk, and is less than 50 feet from the ocean.

The Center is in a VE zone on the current Flood Insurance Rate Map, with a Base Flood Elevation of 13 feet (NAVD88). Its first floor is at 10 feet, and its basement holds the electrical switchgear, boilers, and the emergency generator. Seawater flooded the basement in a 2012 hurricane and again in a 2022 nor’easter. The 2022 event knocked out power to the building for nine days.

The borough has designated the Center as its post-storm supply distribution and recovery center. It’s in good standing with the NFIP and holds flood insurance on the Center. The site isn’t within a Coastal Barrier Resources System unit or an Otherwise Protected Area.

The borough administrator wants to “restore and harden The Rabbits Center for the next century” and “make the boardwalk worthy of it.” The borough plans to apply under the FY2024 & 2025 BRIC NOFO, with applications open March 25 – July 23, 2026. No construction has started on any project below.

### Facts

Raspberry Park Councilmember who used to work as a civil engineer and now serves on the Raspberry Park Council pushes to include a component in the application for a project to replace the rotting bulkhead in front of the Rabbits Center. In addition to the basement construciton project, he wants to use BRIC funding to rebuild the bulkhead, with the same material design, height, and components and repair the bulkhead back to a repaired, functioning state.

## Governing text

Department of Homeland Security (DHS) Notice of Funding Opportunity (NOFO), for FY24 and FY25 “Building Resilient Infrastructure and Communities”

Federal Emergency Management Agency (FEMA)’s Hazard Mitigation Assistance (HMA) Program and Policy Guide, effective January 20, 2025

## Position

The Councilmember states: “Curent bulkhead is rotted and deficient at protecting against potential flooding. Replacing the rotted bulkhead and bringing it back to its former state constitutes an allowable hazard mitigation project, as it is designed to increase resilience and public safety. Repairing it back to its previous state before degradation constitutes an allowable hazard mitigation project under BRIC guidelines.

Goal: Determine if this project design, as proposed by the Councilmember, is eligible for BRIC funding.

## Arguments

### Argument 1

This is a replacement in kind and not eligible for funding under this program. FEMA’s HMA guidance treats repair, deferred maintenance, and replacement of existing infrastructure as ineligible, because it doesn’t increase protection. The resilience language in the NOFO hides that. Excerpt from FEMA HMA Program and Policy Guide, pg. 50: “Projects that, without an increase in the level of protection, address the operation, deferred or future maintenance, rehabilitation, restoration or replacement of existing structures, facilities or infrastructure (e.g., … maintenance/rehabilitation of facilities, including dams and other flood risk reduction structures)” are deemed ineligible for BRIC funding.

### Argument 2

This project would bring the bulkhead up to suitable building codes, and “FEMA recognizes that the adoption and enforcement of modern building codes, specifications, and standards provide significant resilience benefits.” NOFO, pg 15. This project activity of removing rotted bulk and repairing it up to suitable building hazards would constitute allowable capacity-building activities.

### Argument 3

This item of repairing bulkheads is consistent with the New Jersey and Raspberry Park adopted Hazard Mitigation Plan, and would “reduce long term-maintenance costs” for Raspberry Park in maintaining and hardening The Rabbits Center. (FEMA HMA Program and Policy Guide, pg. 92) If maintenance costs were to increase due to this project work, it would no longer be feasibly cost-effective.
